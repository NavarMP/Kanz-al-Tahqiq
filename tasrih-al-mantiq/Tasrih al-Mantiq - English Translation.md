# Tasrīḥ al-Manṭiq — English Translation

In the name of God, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful.

Praise belongs to God, who endowed humankind with articulate speech and fashioned it in a comely form. May blessings and peace be upon the best of those whom He sent with the Book distinguished by eloquent expression, and upon his family and companions, the finest family and companions.

## Assent and Conception (التصديق والتصور)

Know that knowledge—the image formed in the intellect—if it is assent to a declarative relation, is assent; otherwise, it is conception. A relation is the ascription of one thing to another, whether affirmatively or negatively. Assent therefore requires three conceptions: the conception of the subject, the predicate, and the relation, as in assenting to “Zayd is standing”: one must conceive Zayd, conceive “standing,” and conceive the relation between them.

Each of the two—conception and assent—is either immediate or inferential. The immediate is what does not depend on reflection and thought; the inferential is its opposite. In this respect there are four kinds: immediate conception, such as conceiving heat; inferential conception, such as conceiving the reality of jinn; immediate assent, such as assenting that “the sun is shining”; and inferential assent, such as assenting that “the world came into being.”

## Kinds of Signification (أنواع الدلالات)

Signification—whereby knowing one thing entails knowing another—is either verbal or nonverbal. Each of these is conventional if established by the act of a convention-maker, as when “Zayd” signifies the person himself, or the four indicators signify what they indicate; natural if required by nature, as “ah, ah” signifies chest pain, and a rapid pulse signifies fever; or rational if required by reason, as hearing the word “dayz” from behind a wall signifies that someone is speaking, and as a made object signifies the existence of its maker.

The signification relevant here is conventional verbal signification. According to whether a word signifies the whole meaning for which it was assigned, it is correspondence: for example, the word “human” signifies a rational animal. Its signification of part of that meaning—animal alone or rational alone—is inclusion. Its signification of an external concomitant is implication, as when “human” signifies the capacity to write.

## Composite and Simple Expressions (المركب والمفرد)

An expression signifies by correspondence when its part is intended to signify part of its meaning, as in “stone-thrower”; it is then composite. It is complete if it can serve as a complete utterance in communication, and incomplete otherwise. A complete utterance, if it can be true or false, is a report and a proposition. If it cannot, it is performative: if it requests an action, it is a command; if it requests that an action be left, it is a prohibition; if it seeks understanding, it is a question; otherwise, it is a wish, hope, exclamation, or call.

An incomplete expression is restrictive if its second part qualifies its first by a description, as in “rational animal,” or by an annexation, as in “Zayd’s servant.” It is nonrestrictive otherwise, as in “in the house” and “fifteen.” If no part of the expression is intended to signify part of its meaning, it is simple.

A simple expression, if it does not stand independently in meaning, is a particle. If it does stand independently, then if its form signifies a particular time among the three times, it is a verb; otherwise it is a noun. The first is not fit to be either subject or predicate; the second is fit only to be a predicate; and the third is fit to be either.

## Types of Simple Terms by Meaning (أنواع المفرد باعتبار المعنى)

A single expression with one meaning is either such that conceiving it prevents it from applying truly to many individuals—in which case it is a proper name and a particular, like “Zayd”—or it is not. If the meaning applies equally to all its individuals, it is a univocal universal, as the reality of “human” applies equally to each individual—Zayd, ʿAmr, Bakr, and others. Otherwise it is an analogical universal, such as existence: it applies to the Necessary Existent, exalted is He, in a prior and more primary way than it applies to the possible.

If the expression has many meanings, then if it is assigned to all of them equally, it is a homonym, such as “ʿayn” for the eye, a spring, gold, the self, and other meanings. If it is not so assigned, but was assigned to one meaning and transferred to a second because of a relation between them, then if its use in the original assigned meaning has been abandoned and its use in the second has become established, it is a transferred term. If the transfer was made by general custom, it is customary, like “beast,” originally assigned to anything that moves on the earth and transferred to four-legged animals. If it was made by religious law, it is juridical, like “prayer,” originally assigned to supplication and transferred to the prescribed acts. If it was made by a special convention, it is technical, like “verb,” originally assigned to what issues from an agent and transferred to a word that indicates a meaning in itself while being associated with one of the times.

If use of the first meaning has not been abandoned, the expression is literal with respect to that meaning and figurative with respect to the second, as with “lion.” A single expression with multiple meanings is synonymous if the meanings are one, as “layth” and “ghaḍanfar,” or “sitting” and “being seated”; if its meanings are multiple, it is disparate, like “tree” and “stone.”

## The Five Universals (الكليات الخمس)

A universal is either the whole reality of its individuals, as “human” is the whole reality of Zayd, ʿAmr, Bakr, and others. It is then a species: something truly predicated of individuals agreeing in reality, in answer to “What is it?” For example, if one asks, “What are Zayd and ʿAmr?” the answer is “human.” Neither differs from the other except through particular accidents. This is called a real species.

A relative species is defined as a reality of which a genus is predicated, along with another reality, in answer to “What is it?” A relative species is either a real species under a genus, such as “human” under “animal,” or a genus under another genus, such as “animal” under “living body,” “living body” under “body in general,” and that under “substance.” Thus “animal” is predicated of human and horse in answer to “What are they?” Likewise, “living body” is predicated of animal and tree; “body in general” of those and stone; and “substance” of those and intellects. Since “human” satisfies both the definition of a real species and that of a relative species, it is called the lowest species and the species of species. From animal up to body in general, each is only a relative species. Body in general is called the highest species, while what lies between them, such as animal and living body, is an intermediate species.

A universal may instead be part of the reality of its individuals. If it is the whole common element among things with different realities, occurring in answer to “What is it?”—so that any common part between the two realities is not outside that element—then it is a genus. The substantiality, capacity for dimensions, sensitivity, and voluntary movement shared by human and horse are not outside “animal.” Thus “animal” is a genus: if one asks what human and horse are, one answers “animal,” which is the whole of their shared reality.

A genus is a universal predicated of things with different realities in answer to “What is it?” It is proximate if it is the whole common element between a nature and all the things that share that genus; it is remote if it is the whole common element between that nature and only some of those sharers. One reality has several genera, some above others, such as animal, living body, body in general, and substance. Animal is the proximate genus of human: it is the whole common element between humans and horses, donkeys, sheep, cattle, and others. The remaining genera are remote. Animal, as the proximate genus of human, is called the lowest genus; substance, three levels removed, is the highest genus; body in general, two levels removed, and living body, one level removed, are intermediate genera.

If a universal is not the whole common element, then either it is not common at all and distinguishes a reality from those sharing its proximate genus—in which case it is a proximate differentia, like “rational” with respect to human—or it is common but not the whole common element, distinguishing that reality from those sharing its remote genus—in which case it is a remote differentia, like “sensitive” with respect to human. Sensitivity distinguishes human from those sharing “living body,” its remote genus. A differentia is defined as a universal predicated in answer to “What is it?” with respect to its essence, as “rational” of human and “braying” of donkey.

If a universal lies outside the reality of its individuals and is particular to one reality, it is a property. A property is an accidental universal predicated of the individuals of one reality in answer to “What is it?” with respect to its accident, as laughter is predicated of human. If it lies outside and is shared among different realities, it is a common accident: an accidental universal predicated of things with different realities, like walking, shared by human, horse, and others.

Each of these two—property and common accident—is necessary if it cannot be separated from its subject; otherwise it is separable. The necessary property is potential writing with respect to human individuals; the separable property is actual writing. The necessary common accident is potential walking with respect to animal individuals; the separable common accident is actual walking.

## The Four Relations between Universals (النسب الأربع بين الكليات)

If two universals agree in their whole extension in both directions, they are equal, like “human” and “rational”; their contradictories are also equal, like “nonhuman” and “nonrational.” If they agree in their whole extension in only one direction, they stand in absolute generality and specificity, like “animal” and “human”; their contradictories are related in the reverse way, like “non-animal” and “nonhuman.”

If they do not agree wholly in either one direction or both, they stand in a relation of generality and specificity in one respect, like “animal” and “nonhuman,” and “animal” and “white.” If they are wholly disjoint, they are disparate, like “existent” and “nonexistent,” and “human” and “stone.”

Between the contradictories of terms related by generality and specificity in one respect, and the contradictories of disparate terms, there may be complete disjointness, as between “non-animal” and “human,” and between “nonexistent” and “not-nonexistent”; or there may be generality in one respect, as between “non-animal” and “non-white,” and between “nonhuman” and “non-stone.”

## Definition (المعرّف)

A definition of a thing is what, when conceived, gives conception of that thing. It has four kinds. First, the complete definition: a composition of the proximate genus and proximate differentia, as “rational animal” in defining human. Second, the deficient definition: a composition of the remote genus and proximate differentia, as “rational body,” or the proximate differentia alone, as “rational.” Third, the complete description: a composition of the proximate genus and property, as “laughing animal.” Fourth, the deficient description: a composition of the remote genus and property, as “laughing body,” or the property alone, as “laughing.”

## Categorical and Conditional Propositions (القضية الحملية والشرطية)

In the domain of assent, a proposition is an utterance that, by its meaning, admits of truth or falsehood; or an utterance whose speaker may properly be called truthful or false. If its judgment affirms that the predicate belongs to the subject, or denies that it belongs to it, it is categorical, whether affirmative or negative, as “Zayd is standing” and “Zayd is not standing.” Otherwise it is conditional.

In a categorical proposition, the subject and predicate are called the subject and predicate. The word indicating the judgmental relation between them is the copula, which may be temporal or non-temporal, as “was” and “is.” In a conditional proposition, the two parts are called the antecedent and consequent.

A categorical proposition is singular if its subject is particular, as “Zayd is a writer” and “this person is learned.” If its subject is universal, then if the judgment concerns the nature itself, it is natural, as “human is a species.” If the judgment concerns individuals, then if their quantity is specified as all or some, it is quantified—universal or particular. If the quantity is not specified, it is unquantified, as “human is an animal.”

In the sciences, the quantified proposition is the one considered. It has four kinds. The universal affirmative has the quantifiers “every” and the all-inclusive “al-”; examples are “Every human is an animal” and “An animal walks.” The particular affirmative has the quantifiers “some” and “one”; examples are “Some animal is human” and “One of the humans came to me.” The universal negative has the quantifiers “nothing,” “not one,” and an indefinite noun under negation; examples are “Nothing among humans is stone,” “Not one of them is inanimate,” and “There is no man in the house.” The particular negative has the forms “not all,” “not some,” and “some are not”; examples are “Not every animal is human,” “Not some of them are human,” and “Some of them are not human.”

## The Negated-Term Proposition (القضية المعدولة)

A negation particle such as “not” or “is not” may be made part of a term. If it is part of the subject, the proposition is a negated-subject proposition, as “The nonliving is inanimate.” If it is part of the predicate, it is a negated-predicate proposition, as “Inanimate matter is nonliving.” If it is part of both, it is a double-negated proposition, as “The nonliving is not learned.”

## Modal Propositions (القضايا الموجهة)

Know that, in reality, the relation of predicate to subject—whether affirmative or negative—is qualified by some mode, such as necessity, permanence, possibility, impossibility, and other modes. A proposition qualified by these real modes is called modal, and the expression that indicates its mode is called its modality. Such a proposition is either simple, whose reality is solely affirmative or solely negative, or compound, whose reality combines affirmation and negation.

### Simple Modal Propositions (القضايا البسيطة)

There are eight simple modal propositions.

1. The absolute necessary proposition: one in which the predicate is judged necessarily to belong to, or be denied of, the subject for as long as the subject itself exists. Example: “Every human is necessarily an animal”; “Nothing among humans is necessarily stone.”
2. The general conditioned proposition: one in which the predicate is judged necessarily to belong to, or be denied of, the subject for as long as the subject has the defining description. Example: “Every writer’s fingers necessarily move as long as he is writing”; “Nothing among writers has motionless fingers necessarily while he is writing.”
3. The absolute temporal proposition: one in which the predicate is judged necessarily to belong to, or be denied of, the subject at a specified time in the subject’s existence. Example: “Every moon is necessarily eclipsed when the earth comes between it and the sun”; “No moon is necessarily eclipsed at the time of quadrature.”
4. The absolute intermittent proposition: one in which affirmation or denial is judged necessary at some time. Example: “Every human necessarily breathes at some time”; “Nothing among humans necessarily fails to breathe at some time.”
5. The absolute permanent proposition: one in which the predicate is judged to remain affirmed or denied for as long as the subject itself exists. Example: “Every celestial sphere is always moving”; “Nothing among them is always stationary.” The difference between necessity and permanence is that necessity means the relation cannot be severed, whereas permanence means it is not severed across times and periods. Permanence is broader: the severance need not be impossible in itself, as with the permanent motion of the celestial sphere.
6. The general customary proposition: one in which affirmation or denial remains for as long as the description holds. Example: “Every writer’s fingers move as long as he is writing”; “Nothing among writers has motionless fingers as long as he is writing.”
7. The general absolute proposition: one in which affirmation or denial holds actually—that is, in one of the three times. Example: “Every human is actually laughing”; “No human is actually laughing.”
8. The general possible proposition: one in which necessity is denied of the relation on the opposite side. Example: “Every human can write in the general sense of possibility”; “No human can write in the general sense of possibility.”

## Compound Modal Propositions (القضايا المركبة)

Compound propositions are made from two propositions, one of which is expressly stated. They differ in affirmation and negation, but agree in universality and particularity, making seven kinds. Since whether the compound is affirmative or negative depends on its first part, two examples are given for each.

1. The special conditioned proposition is the general conditioned proposition restricted by non-permanence in itself. Example: “Every writer’s fingers necessarily move as long as he is writing, but not always”—affirmative; “Nothing among writers’ fingers is necessarily still as long as they are writing, but not always”—negative.
2. The temporal proposition is the absolute temporal proposition restricted by non-permanence in itself. Example: “Every moon is necessarily eclipsed when the earth comes between it and the sun, but not always”—affirmative; “No moon is necessarily eclipsed at quadrature, but not always”—negative.
3. The intermittent proposition is the absolute intermittent proposition restricted by non-permanence in itself. Example: “Every human necessarily breathes at some time, but not always”—affirmative; “Nothing among humans necessarily breathes at some time, but not always”—negative.
4. The special customary proposition is the general customary proposition with the restriction of non-permanence in itself. Example: “Every writer’s fingers move as long as he is writing, but not always”—affirmative; “Nothing among writers’ fingers is still as long as they are writing, but not always”—negative.
5. The existential non-necessary proposition is the general absolute proposition restricted by non-necessity in itself. Example: “Every human actually laughs, but not necessarily”—affirmative; “No human actually laughs, but not necessarily”—negative.
6. The existential non-permanent proposition is the general absolute proposition restricted by non-permanence in itself. Example: “Every human actually laughs, but not always”—affirmative; “No human actually laughs, but not always”—negative.
7. The special possible proposition is one in which necessity in itself is denied for both the agreeing and opposing sides. It is therefore composed of two general possibilities. Example: “Every human can write in the special sense of possibility”; “No human can write in the special sense of possibility.” That is, “Every human can write in the general sense of possibility” and “No human can write in the general sense of possibility” on the affirmative side, and the reverse on the negative side. The difference between the affirmative and negative forms is only verbal.

## Connected and Disconnected Conditional Propositions (القضايا المتصلة والمنفصلة)

A chapter on conditional propositions: if the judgment in a conditional proposition is that the consequent necessarily follows from the antecedent, or that it does not, the proposition is a necessary connected conditional. Its affirmative form is “Whenever the sun rises, daylight exists”; its negative form is “It is not at all the case that whenever the sun rises, night exists.”

If the judgment is that the consequent happens to accompany the antecedent, or that the two do not happen to coincide, it is an accidental connected conditional. The affirmative example is “Whenever a human is rational, a horse neighs”; the negative example is “It is not at all the case that whenever a human is rational, a donkey brays.”

If the judgment is that the two relations cannot both be true or both false, or denies this, the proposition is a true disjunctive conditional. Its affirmative example is “This number is either even or odd”; its negative example is “It is not at all the case that this number is either even or divisible into two equal parts.”

If the two parts cannot both be true, it is an exclusive disjunction. The affirmative example is “This thing is either a tree or a stone”; the negative example is “It is not at all the case that Zayd is either human or rational.”

If the two parts cannot both be false, it is an inclusive disjunction. The affirmative example is “Zayd is either in the sea or does not drown”; the negative example is “It is not at all the case that this thing is either a tree or a stone.”

Each of these three disjunctive kinds is adversarial if its incompatibility is judged by the nature of the two parts, as the incompatibility of evenness and oddness. If the incompatibility is not due to their natures but to mere coincidence, as the incompatibility between a human’s being black and being a writer, it is coincidental.

## Specified, Quantified, and Unquantified Conditional Propositions (الشرطية المخصوصة والمحـصورة والمهملة)

A conditional proposition is specified if its judgment applies under a particular circumstance, as “If you visit me today, I will honor you,” and “On this day Zayd will either die or recover.”

Otherwise, if the consequent’s following from the antecedent, or its incompatibility with it, or the denial of either, holds under every supposition of the antecedent, it is universally quantified. Examples: “Whenever the sun rises, daylight exists”; “The number is always either even or odd”; “It is never the case that whenever the sun rises, night exists”; and “It is never the case that the number is either even or divisible into two equal parts.”

If it holds under only some suppositions, it is particular. Examples: “Sometimes, if a thing is an animal, it is human”; “Sometimes, a thing is either a stone or a tree”; “Sometimes it is not the case that if a thing is an animal, it is human”; and “Sometimes a thing is neither that the sun is rising nor that daylight exists.”

If the suppositions are left unspecified, the proposition is unquantified, as “If a thing is human, it is an animal,” and “A number is either even or odd.” The quantifiers of the universal affirmative connected proposition are “whenever,” “whatever,” and “every time”; of the universal affirmative disjunctive proposition, “always” and “ever.” The quantifier of a universal negative, connected or disjunctive, is “never at all.” The particular affirmative connected and disjunctive quantifier is “sometimes”; their particular negative quantifier is “sometimes not.”

## Contradiction (التناقض)

A chapter: Contradiction is the difference between two propositions such that, by their very nature, the truth of either entails the falsehood of the other. They must differ in affirmation and negation, and in universality and particularity, while agreeing in eight respects: unity of subject; unity of predicate; unity of place and time, as in “Zayd is standing in the market at noon” and “Zayd is not standing in the market at noon”; unity of potentiality or actuality, as in “Zayd is writing actually or potentially” and “Zayd is not writing actually or potentially”; unity of condition, as in “The world is bright provided that daylight exists” and “The world is not bright provided that daylight exists”; unity of relation, as in “Zayd is the father of ʿAmr” and “Zayd is not the father of ʿAmr”; and unity of part and whole, as in “The whole or part of the Zanj is black” and “The whole or part of the Zanj is not black.”

The contradictory of a universal affirmative is a particular negative; the contradictory of a universal negative is a particular affirmative, and conversely. For example: “Every human is an animal” and “Some human is not an animal”; “Nothing among animals is stone” and “Some animal is stone.”

## Simple Conversion (العكس المستوي)

A chapter: Simple conversion is reversing the two terms of a proposition, placing one where the other stood, while preserving truth—so that if the original is assumed true, its converse is true accordingly—and preserving quality: if the original is affirmative or negative, so is the converse.

A universal or particular affirmative converts to a particular affirmative. Thus the converse of “Every human is an animal” and “Some human is an animal” is “Some animal is human.” A universal negative converts to a universal negative: the converse of “Nothing among humans is stone” is “Nothing among stones is human.” A particular negative does not convert at all—not to a universal nor to a particular.

## Conversion by Contraposition (عكس النقيض)

A chapter: Conversion by contraposition reverses the two terms of a proposition by placing the contradictory of the second first and the contradictory of the first second, while preserving truth and quality. The contraposition of a universal affirmative is universal affirmative. For example, “Every human is an animal” converts to “Every non-animal is nonhuman.” A particular affirmative has no contraposition.

A negative proposition, whether universal or particular, converts to a particular negative. Thus the contraposition of “Nothing among humans is horse” and “Some human is not a horse” is “Some non-horse is not nonhuman.”

## Syllogism (القياس)

A chapter: A syllogism is an utterance composed of propositions from which another utterance follows by their very nature. If its conclusion is explicitly present in it as such, or as its contradictory, it is an exception syllogism; otherwise it is a conjunctive syllogism. If it is composed purely of categorical propositions, it is categorical; otherwise it is conditional.

In a categorical syllogism, the subject of the conclusion is called the minor term and its predicate the major term. The proposition containing the minor term is the minor premise; the one containing the major term is the major premise. The repeated term between minor and major is called the middle term. The arrangement formed by the two premises is called a figure.

If the middle term is predicate in the minor premise and subject in the major premise, the figure is the first. If it is predicate in both, it is the second. If it is subject in both, it is the third. If it is subject in the minor premise and predicate in the major premise, it is the fourth.

## The First Figure (الشكل الأول)

The first figure requires the minor premise to be affirmative and the major premise to be universal. It has four productive moods. First: two universal affirmatives produce a universal affirmative: “Every human is an animal; every animal is sensitive; therefore every human is sensitive.” Second: a universal affirmative minor and a universal negative major produce a universal negative: “Every human is an animal; no animal is inanimate; therefore no human is inanimate.” Third: a particular affirmative minor and a universal affirmative major produce a particular affirmative: “Some animal is human; every human is rational; therefore some animal is rational.” Fourth: a particular affirmative minor and a universal negative major produce a particular negative: “Some animal is human; no human is a donkey; therefore some animal is not a donkey.”

## The Second Figure (الشكل الثاني)

The second figure requires one premise to be affirmative and the other negative, with the major premise universal. It has four productive moods. First: a universal affirmative minor and a universal negative major produce a universal negative: “Every human is an animal; no stone is an animal; therefore no human is stone.” Second: a universal negative minor and a universal affirmative major also produce a universal negative: “No stone is an animal; every human is an animal; therefore no stone is human.” Third: a particular affirmative minor and a universal negative major produce a particular negative: “Some animal is human; no stone is human; therefore some animal is not stone.” Fourth: a particular negative minor and a universal affirmative major also produce a particular negative: “Some animal is not human; every rational being is human; therefore some animal is not rational.”

## The Third Figure (الشكل الثالث)

The third figure requires the minor premise to be affirmative and at least one of the two premises to be universal. It has six productive moods. The first three are: two universal affirmatives; a particular affirmative minor with a universal affirmative major; and a universal affirmative minor with a particular affirmative major. Each produces a particular affirmative.

Examples: “Every human is an animal; every human is rational; therefore some animal is rational.” “Some animal is human; every animal is a body; therefore some human is a body.” “Every human is an animal; some human is rational; therefore some animal is rational.”

The fourth, fifth, and sixth moods are: a universal affirmative minor with a universal negative major; a particular affirmative minor with a universal negative major; and a universal affirmative minor with a particular negative major. Each produces a particular negative.

Examples: “Every human is an animal; no human is a horse; therefore some animal is not a horse.” “Some human is an animal; no human is a donkey; therefore some animal is not a donkey.” “Every human is an animal; some human is not a horse; therefore some animal is not a horse.”

## The Fourth Figure (الشكل الرابع)

The fourth figure requires one of two conditions: either both premises are affirmative and the minor is universal, or they differ in quality and one of them is universal. It has eight productive moods.

First: two universal affirmatives. Second: a universal affirmative minor and a particular affirmative major. Both produce a particular affirmative. Examples: “Every human is an animal; every rational being is human; therefore some animal is rational”; and “Every human is rational; some writer is human; therefore some rational being is a writer.”

Third: a universal negative minor and a universal affirmative major produce a universal negative: “No animal is stone; every human is an animal; therefore no stone is human.”

The fourth is a universal affirmative minor with a universal negative major. The fifth is a particular affirmative minor with a universal negative major. The sixth is a particular negative minor with a universal affirmative major. The seventh is a universal affirmative minor with a particular negative major. The eighth is a universal negative minor with a particular affirmative major. These five moods produce a particular negative.

Examples: “Every human is an animal; no stone is human; therefore some animal is not stone.” “Some human is an animal; no stone is human; therefore some animal is not stone.” “Some human is not stone; every rational being is human; therefore some stone is not rational.” “Every human is an animal; some stone is not human; therefore some animal is not stone.” “Nothing among humans is stone; some rational being is human; therefore some stone is not rational.”

## Conditional Syllogism (القياس الشرطي)

A conditional syllogism is composed either of two connected propositions, as “Whenever Zayd is human, he is an animal; whenever he is an animal, he is a body; therefore whenever Zayd is human, he is a body”; or of two disjunctive propositions, as “This number is either even or odd; every even number is either an even-even or an even-odd; therefore every number is either odd, even-even, or even-odd”; or of a categorical proposition and a connected proposition, as “This thing is human; whenever a thing is human, it is an animal; therefore this thing is an animal”; or of a categorical proposition and a disjunctive proposition, as “This is a number; the number is always either even or odd; therefore this is either even or odd”; or of a connected proposition and a disjunctive proposition, as “Whenever this is three, it is a number; the number is always either even or odd; therefore whenever this is three, it is either even or odd.” These are five divisions, and the four figures arise in them as they do in categorical syllogisms. A full treatment is lengthy and is found in larger works.

## Exception Syllogism (القياس الاستثنائي)

An exception syllogism is either composed of a necessary connected proposition, in which affirming the antecedent affirms the consequent and denying the consequent denies the antecedent. Example: “If this body is human, it is an animal; but it is human; therefore it is an animal. But it is not an animal; therefore it is not human.”

Or it is composed of a true disjunctive proposition, in which affirming either part denies the other, and conversely. Example: “This number is either even or odd; but it is even; therefore it is not odd. But it is odd; therefore it is not even. But it is not odd; therefore it is even. But it is not even; therefore it is odd.”

Or it is composed of an exclusive disjunction, in which affirming one part denies the other. Example: “This is either a tree or a stone; but it is a tree; therefore it is not a stone. But it is a stone; therefore it is not a tree.”

Or it is composed of an inclusive disjunction, in which denying one part affirms the other. Example: “This is either not a tree or not a stone; but it is not the case that it is not a tree; therefore it is not a stone. But it is not the case that it is not a stone; therefore it is not a tree.”

## Induction (الاستقراء)

A chapter: Induction is an argument in which a judgment about particulars is used to infer a judgment about their universal. It is complete if all particulars are examined to establish a universal judgment. For example: “Every elemental body is either an animal, a plant, or an inanimate thing; each of these occupies space; therefore every elemental body occupies space.”

It is incomplete if most particulars are examined and the judgment is made about all, as in “Every animal moves its lower jaw while chewing.” We have observed that this is so in humans, birds, beasts, and the other animals we have examined. Such a judgment does not yield certainty, since there may be an animal that is not so, as is reported of the crocodile.

## Analogy (التمثيل)

A chapter: Analogy is an argument that infers from the state of one particular to another particular on account of something shared between them. Its best-known methods are two.

The first is correlation: a judgment accompanies a description capable of serving as a cause, both when the description exists and when it is absent. Whenever the description is present, the judgment is present; whenever the description is absent, the judgment is absent. An example is the prohibition of wine accompanying intoxication: as long as it intoxicates, it is forbidden; when the intoxication is gone, the prohibition is gone.

The second is elimination by alternatives: examining the attributes of the original case and invalidating some of them so that the remaining one is identified as the cause. For example, one says that the cause of wine’s prohibition is either its being made from grapes, its fluidity, its color, its taste, its smell, or its intoxicating effect. But none of these except intoxication can serve as the cause, since each of the others exists in other things without prohibition. Intoxication is therefore identified as the cause of the prohibition.

## The Five Arts (الصناعات الخمس)

A chapter on the five arts. The first is demonstration: an argument composed of certainties. Among them are axioms, self-evident propositions for which conceiving both terms and their relation is enough to yield conviction, such as “The whole is greater than the part.” Another kind is the innate inferences: propositions that require an intermediary that does not escape the mind when the terms are present, such as “Four is even.”

Among them are observations: propositions judged by the intellect through either an external sense, in which case they are sense-perceptions, such as “The sun is shining”; or an internal sense, in which case they are inner experiences, such as “We feel hunger and thirst.” Another kind is intuitive judgments: the mind’s immediate passage from premises to a conclusion, as in “The moon’s light is derived from the sun’s light.”

Among them are experimental judgments: repeating an act until conviction is reached, such as “Scammony purges yellow bile.” Another kind is mass-transmitted reports: reports by a group whose agreement on a lie is rationally impossible, such as “Mecca exists.”

An argument composed of these is a demonstrative proof quia when its middle term is a cause of the relation in reality, just as it is a cause of it in the mind. Example: “This person has corrupted humors; everyone with corrupted humors is feverish; therefore this person is feverish.” It is a demonstrative proof propter quid if the middle term is not the cause of the judgment in reality. Example: “This person is feverish; every feverish person has corrupted humors; therefore this person has corrupted humors.”

The second art is dialectic, composed of commonly accepted propositions, such as “Justice is good” and “Oppression is vile,” or of conceded premises, such as conceding that a command indicates obligation.

The third is rhetoric, composed of accepted beliefs taken from someone in whom people place trust, such as saints, or of conjectures, such as judging that rain will fall when clouds appear.

The fourth is poetry, composed of imaginings to which the soul yields and by which it is moved through encouragement or warning—for example, when a poem is recited in a pleasing or unpleasant voice, or in a good or poor meter.

The fifth is sophistry, composed of fanciful premises, as in “Everything that exists is pointed to” and “Everything that exists occupies space,” or of deceptive likenesses, as when an image of a donkey painted on a wall is called a donkey, and it is said, “Every donkey brays.” Its purpose is only to confuse the opponent.

This is the end of what I have extracted from the books of this discipline, to make the study of sound reasoning easier, naming it *Tasrīḥ*. Praise belongs to God, Lord of all worlds. May blessings and peace be upon the best of His creation, Muhammad, his family, and all his companions.
